This rule is mainly about subject–verb agreement with quantity expressions. 
The key is that some expressions take a plural noun + plural verb, while many a and more than one take a singular noun + singular verb.

                                     1. Many + plural noun + plural verb

Many **girls** **go** to college on time.

many → quantity word
girls → plural noun
go → plural verb

So:

Many + plural noun + plural verb


                                      2. A many / A number + plural noun + plural verb

Examples:

A great many **students** **have** been declared successful.

A number of **students are** absent.

So:

A many / A number + plural noun + plural verb

Example

❌ A great many student have been declared successful.

✅ A great many **students have** been declared successful.


                                        3. Many a + singular noun + singular verb ⭐

This is very important.

Many a **girl goes** to college on time.

Here:

many a → singular construction
girl → singular
goes → singular verb

So:

Many a + singular noun + singular verb

Another example:

Many a man has come to India from Canada.

                                                    4. The many / The number

These are also important.

**The many + plural noun + plural verb**

The many problems are difficult to solve.(Here the many is adjective so verb agrees with main noun problems)

**The number + plural noun + singular verb**

The number of students is increasing.(Here the number is the main noun, as number is singular verb used is singular)

Notice:

The number of students is...

Not:

❌ The number of students are...


                                                    5. More than one ⭐

This is an important exception.
Structure:

**More than one + singular noun + singular verb**

**More than one** room **is** vacant.

Not:

❌ More than one rooms are vacant.



                                                      6. More than two

With more than two, use a plural noun and plural verb.

More than two rooms are vacant.

So:

More than one room is vacant. ✅

More than two rooms are vacant. ✅

                                                    7. More rooms than one

This is another pattern from the lesson:

**More rooms than one are vacant.**

Here rooms is plural, so the verb is plural.





                                                         | Expression                        | Noun     | Verb         |
                                                        | --------------------------------- | -------- | ------------ |
                                                        | **Many + noun**                   | Plural   | Plural       |
                                                        | **A great many + noun**           | Plural   | Plural       |
                                                        | **A number of + noun**            | Plural   | Plural       |
                                                        | **Many a + noun**                 | Singular | Singular     |
                                                        | **The many + noun**               | Plural   | Plural       |
                                                        | **The number of + noun**          | Plural   | **Singular** |
                                                        | **More than one + noun**          | Singular | Singular     |
                                                        | **More than two + noun**          | Plural   | Plural       |
                                                        | **More + plural noun + than one** | Plural   | Plural       |



"**The number of students is increasing.**"
Here, "the number" refers to a specific, particular figure — you're talking about the number itself going up or down. "Number" is the real subject, and it's singular. Think of it as: that specific count is increasing.

"**A number of students are absent.**"
Here, "a number of" doesn't mean "the count" at all — idiomatically, it just means "several" or "many." It's not functioning as a noun (the actual subject); it's functioning as a quantifier, exactly like "many" or "some." So:

A number of students are absent.
= Many students are absent. / Several students are absent.

Since "a number of" = "many/several," the verb agrees with the noun that follows ("students" → plural → "are"), not with "number."

Why the definite/indefinite article changes everything:

"The" number → points to one specific, identifiable count → singular subject
"A" number of → means "some/many" → acts as a quantifier, not a subject → verb agrees with the plural noun after it
